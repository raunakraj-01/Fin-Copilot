Abstract—A student profile platform must reconcile assessment 
records, portfolio evidence, and the different responsibilities of 
institutional users. This paper examines an implementation that 
combines these functions through a React interface, an Express 
application, and a PostgreSQL academic ledger. Student, teacher, 
administrator, and placement views share the same records while 
applying role and assignment checks at selected server routes. The 
platform also includes assessment trend analysis, credit -weighted 
academic history, evidence -backed candidate matching, and 
optional local language -model assistance. We trace the active 
execution path and evaluate numerical behavior using the two 
repository unit tests and 16 supplementary function checks. All 
checks pass ag ainst their stated expectations. Constructed 
examples reproduce increasing, decreasing, and constant score 
trends, while exposing differences in missing -mark treatment 
between services. A separate rubric example shows that the 
deterministic fallback can as sign half marks to unrelated 
nonempty text. These findings establish reproducible 
implementation behavior and identify the validation work 
required before institutional use. The study does not measure 
learning outcomes, model accuracy, deployment latency, or 
security effectiveness. 
Index Terms—academic analytics, student profiles, role -based 
access control, evidence provenance, assessment management, 
local language models 
I. INTRODUCTION 
An academic profile contains records with different 
meanings and different levels of authority. A teacher -entered 
mark contributes to an assessment ledger. An uploaded 
marksheet may supply an official grade -point value. A project 
description records a stud ent’s claim, while a synchronized 
repository provides an external observation. Combining these 
items requires explicit rules about ownership, calculation, and 
evidence. A dashboard can otherwise present values together 
even when their underlying assumptions differ. 
The implementation examined here connects academic and 
portfolio records in a shared web application. Students manage 
profiles and inspect their records. Teachers enter assessments 
and examine assigned groups. Administrators manage 
institutional data and visibility settings. Placement staff inspect 
candidate evidence and compare it with job requirements. The 
design includes optional local language -model support for 
selected tasks, alongside deterministic calculations that operate 
without a model response. 
The engineering question is whether these functions preserve 
interpretable record semantics across their service boundaries. 
Missing work, a recorded zero, and an unassessed submission 
require different treatment. Likewise, an extrapolated score and 
a veri fied academic result have different evidential status. 
These distinctions affect the interpretation of every chart and 
recommendation. 
II. RELATED WORK 
A. Educational data mining and learning dashboards 
Using student data to understand learning is not a new idea. 
Baker and Yacef [1] followed the growth of educational data 
mining as it separated from general data mining and became a 
field of its own, and they described how its attention moved 
from simple d escription of student behaviour towards 
prediction, clustering and the discovery of relationships 
between variables. Romero and Ventura [2] later revisited the 
field and showed how much wider the tools, data sources and 
applications had become by 2020. Tak en together, these 
surveys show that the community has been good at asking what 
can be learned from academic data. They say much less about 
a quieter question that matters once the data reaches a screen: 
does a number mean the same thing everywhere it is shown? 
Dashboards are where that question becomes practical. 
Verbert et al. [3] examined a set of learning analytics dashboard 
applications and compared what they put in front of teachers 
and learners, such as activity, time spent and resource use. A 
chart tends to look authoritative, so a choice hidden behind it, 
for example how a missing mark is counted, is easy to overlook. 
Our results on pending assessments and denominators sit in 

exactly this gap between a clean visual and the calculation 
underneath it. We do not suggest that the cited work is wrong 
about dashboards. It simply was not written to check individual 
calculations. 
B. Ethics, feedback and teacher judgement 
Analytics about students is never only a technical matter. 
Slade and Prinsloo [4] discuss the ethical side of learning 
analytics, including consent, privacy, and the risk that students 
are categorised on the basis of partial data. That concern applies 
directly to any system that extrapolates scores, because a 
predicted next value can easily be read as a statement about the 
student rather than about a short sequence of numbers. For this 
reason we describe our regression output only as a descriptive 
continuation of the supplied records. 
Research on feedback points the same way. Hattie and 
Timperley [5] argue that feedback helps most when it is 
specific, tied to the task, and tells the learner what to do next, 
and that a bare score does much less. Panadero and Jönsson [6] 
reach a related conclusion about rubrics: their educational value 
depends on how they are used, for instance alongside feedback 
and self -assessment, and not on their mere presence. A 
keyword-based fallback cannot offer either of these things. This 
is why the platform keeps suggested marks apart from reviewed 
marks, and why we argue that the final decision should stay 
with the teacher. 
C. Access control and provenance 
A shared platform for students, teachers, administrators and 
placement staff raises an access question from the start: who 
may see which record? Sandhu et al. [7] described role -based 
access control, in which permissions attach to roles and users 
are assigned to those roles. Ferraiolo et al. [8] later proposed a 
standard model for it through NIST. A role is a coarse 
permission, though. It says what kind of user is asking, not 
whether this particular user should see this particular record. In 
practice it has to be paired with checks on the requested object, 
and the lack of such checks in a few routes is one of the 
weaknesses we report. 
Provenance deals with a neighbouring problem, which is 
knowing where a piece of data came from. Buneman et al. [9] 
separate the question of why a value appears in a result from 
the question of where it was copied from. For a student profile 
this has a plai n consequence. A faculty -verified grade, a 
synchronized repository and a self -written project description 
do not deserve equal trust, and the interface should show which 
of them it is displaying. 
D. Language models in education 
Large language models have found a natural place in 
education, and some obvious risks have come with them. 
Kasneci et al. [10] describe opportunities for teaching and 
assessment but stress fact checking and human oversight, partly 
because learners may trust a fluent answer too readily. Ji et al. 
[11] explain part of the reason: generated text can read 
smoothly while saying things the source does not support. 
Small models that run locally sharpen this trade -off. The 
Qwen2.5 family [12] is released in several sizes, including a 3-
billion-parameter version that can run on modest hardware. 
That makes it attractive for institutions that prefer not to send 
student data to an outside service. A smaller model also has less 
capacity, however, so its output needs care ful checking before 
anyone relies on it. 
Reproducibility is the last strand. Kapoor and Narayanan 
[13] collected cases in which data leakage inflated the reported 
performance of machine -learning studies across many fields. 
This is one reason we do not claim any predictive accuracy 
from our constructed examples. 
E. Research gap 
The work above covers analytics methods, ethics, access 
control, provenance and language models, mostly one at a time. 
What is seldom examined is how these concerns behave 
together inside a single working codebase, and whether the 
same percentage means the  same thing in every service that 
reports it. This paper addresses that gap through source tracing 
and executed function-level checks. 
III. IMPLEMENTATION SCOPE AND ARCHITECTURE 
A. Execution path and system boundary 
The evaluated platform follows a three -tier architecture 
comprising a React and TypeScript presentation layer, an 
Express application layer, and PostgreSQL persistence. During 
startup, the backend initializes the academic database, 
schedules external -profile synchronization, mounts six role -
scoped API groups, and serves the web interface through 
development middleware or an optimized production build. 
The evaluation follows the active application path and 
considers only components reachable during normal execution, 
preventing obsolete or unused modules from influencing the 
reported findings. 
Fig. 1 shows the request path shared by the four application 
roles. Credentials enter JWT validation, after which role and 
record-scope checks determine the accessible dashboard. The 
implementation uses role -specific React views, Express route 
groups, PostgreSQL services, optional local Ollama requests, 
and external-profile synchronization. 

 
Fig. 1. Role-based workflow from authentication to scope-limited dashboards. The diagram shows intended access paths; it does not assert complete authorization 
coverage. 
B. Roles and access decisions 
The authentication middleware verifies a bearer token, 
reloads the corresponding account, rejects inactive accounts, 
and refreshes the student identifier where applicable. Route 
middleware restricts teacher endpoints to the teacher role. 
Several handlers t hen check a teacher’s class, subject, and 
semester assignment before querying or modifying marks. 
Fig. 2 separates authentication from the record -level 
decisions required for academic data. A request is accepted 
only after token, role, assignment, and ownership checks 
establish a scoped query; a failed check returns an authorization 
error before data retrieval. 
 
Fig. 2. Authorization sequence for a record-scoped request. The dashed branch 
represents rejection with an HTTP 401 or 403 response. 
These mechanisms describe the intended access model. 
Their presence does not prove complete authorization 
coverage. The rubric -read, submission -read, and AI -marking 
handlers accept an assessment identifier without performing the 
same assignment check used by the principal marks -entry 
route. Their route-level role check therefore needs an additional 
record-level authorization review. The schema includes 
colleges and departments, but this study does not establish 
isolation between separate institutional tenants. 
C. Operational separation 
The application separates profile synchronization, academic 
computation, and optional generation. A synchronization 
failure should retain an explicit unavailable state rather than 
imply a lack of student activity. Model availability is also 
separate from l edger availability. The role intelligence engine 
has deterministic responses, while some fallback paths report 
that local AI is unavailable. This distinction allows the interface 
to explain why a narrative is absent without replacing a 
recorded academic fact. 
IV. DATA MODEL AND EVIDENCE SEMANTICS 
A. Academic records 
The schema links users to student profiles, classes, subjects, 
semesters, teacher assignments, and marks. Assessment 
definitions add maximum marks, dates, weights, answer keys, 
and rubric criteria. Mark entries can retain suggested scores, 
teacher feedback , review information, and publication state. 
These relationships support the scoped authorization sequence 
in Fig. 2 and the analytics pipeline in Fig. 3. 
The marking service represents absence and pending work 
through explicit statuses. It stores null marks for several 
missing-work states and stores zero for the zero state. The batch 
save routine checks class membership and numeric bounds, 
then writes accepted entries inside a database transaction. Rows 
rejected during application validation can be skipped while 
accepted rows commit. A database exception triggers rollback. 
This is a partially accepted batch policy, not an all -or-nothing 
validation policy. 
B. Provenance and freshness 
The evidence service distinguishes self -reported 
information, externally retrieved records, and faculty -verified 
material. It carries verification and freshness fields, together 
with source links and missing -information descriptions. These 
fields help a re viewer decide how much weight to place on a 
profile item. Tracking the origin and derivation of records 
addresses the provenance concerns described by Buneman et 
al. [9]. 


The platform’s evidence labels require careful interpretation. 
A repository language or description match is an observation 
about an artifact. It does not establish a student’s proficiency. 
In the candidate -matching implementation, such a match can 
contribute to a DEMONSTRATED requirement classification. 
That label should be read as a result of the implemented 
matching rule, pending technical review of the underlying 
artifact. 
C. Academic authority 
Academic history distinguishes calculated grade -point 
values from positive official values stored with uploaded 
marksheets. The returned record includes flags describing this 
choice. The service tracks failed subject attempts and the latest 
recorded attemp t for each subject when counting active 
backlogs. An absent external profile or an unavailable model 
response supplies no evidence about academic eligibility. 
V. ANALYTICAL AND ASSISTANCE METHODS 
Fig. 3 traces the academic analytics path from mark entry to 
teacher review. Status and numeric validation precede ledger 
storage. Aggregation policy then determines the statistics and 
trends presented in role -scoped dashboards, so missing -mark 
and weighting rules influence every downstream result. 
 
Fig. 3. Academic analytics pipeline. Validation and aggregation policy are 
explicit stages between record entry and intervention. 
A. Score aggregation and grade points 
For a recorded mark mi with a positive maximum Mi, the 
teacher analytics route computes 
 pi = 100 mi / Mi,     p̄  = (1/n) Σi=1n pi (1) 
This mean gives each assessment percentage equal weight. 
The class -subject service first aggregates obtained and 
maximum marks for each assessed student, then averages the 
resulting student percentages. The academic -history service 
aggregates marks by subj ect and semester. These definitions 
agree only under particular distributions of maxima and 
missing records. For subject credit cj and grade point gj, the 
calculated semester grade-point average is 
 G = (Σj cj gj) / (Σj cj). (2) 
The academic service retrieves institutional rules and 
otherwise uses its embedded grading scale. A positive official 
marksheet value takes precedence over the calculated value. 
Although assessment definitions contain weights, this 
aggregation path sums obtained and maximum marks directly; 
it does not apply those stored weights.

B. Longitudinal score trends 
The regression helper fits a straight line to points (xi, pi) using 
ordinary least squares: 
 a = (n Σi xipi − Σi xi Σi pi) / (n Σi xi2 − (Σi xi)2), (3) 
 b = p̄  − ax̄ . 
It returns no fit for fewer than two points or a zero 
denominator. The next value is a(maxi xi + 1) + b, clamped to 
[0, 100]. The helper returns the coefficient of determination R2. 
For a constant response series, its explicit convention is R2 = 1. 
The teacher route builds single -student points from marks 
ordered by creation time. Cohort points use averages grouped 
by exam type in first -encounter order. The horizontal axis is 
therefore a record or milestone index. It does not measure 
elapsed time. A trend may reflect assessment order, difficulty, 
or selection, so the extrapolation is a descriptive continuation 
of the supplied sequence. 
C. Rubric suggestions and teacher review 
The marking service constructs a prompt from submission 
content, a teacher -defined rubric, and an optional answer key. 
In the configuration examined here, the optional local model is 
Qwen2.5:3B served through Ollama [12]. A usable local 
response supplies p er-criterion suggestions that the code caps 
to criterion maxima. Submitted code is statically inspected 
through text; this path does not execute it. The data model 
retains suggestions separately from reviewed marks and 
published feedback. 
When the model call fails or its result is unusable, a 
deterministic fallback counts rubric -description keywords 
longer than four characters. If q is the fraction of matched 
keywords and R is the criterion maximum, a nonempty 
response receives approximately 
 s = R max(0.5, q), (4) 
rounded to one decimal place. An empty submission returns 
zero. If the criterion has no qualifying keywords, the fallback 
uses q = 0.6. Keyword coverage provides a reproducible score 
but cannot establish conceptual correctness. 
D. Candidate requirement matching 
The placement service evaluates grade -point, backlog, and 
branch conditions separately from portfolio alignment. For D 
demonstrated requirements, P partially met requirements, and 
K requirements in total, it computes 
 A = round(100 (D + 0.5P) / max(K, 1)). (5) 
The result is capped at 100. It is a rule-based alignment index 
and has no measured interpretation as a hiring probability. The 
inspected matcher permits an unknown CGPA to pass its 
CGPA condition. An institution would need an explicit 
eligibility policy f or that case before using the result in a 
shortlist. 
VI. REPRODUCIBLE CODE-LEVEL EVALUATION 
A. Protocol and evidence boundaries 
The evaluation executes the repository’s two regression unit 
tests and 16 supplementary checks against the pinned source 
snapshot using Node.js 24.11.1. The supplementary harness 
strips TypeScript annotations and substitutes controlled query 
responses for database access. The rubric examples force an 
unsuccessful model response to exercise the fallback. The 
application source remains unchanged. 
Table II defines the coverage. All supplementary checks pass 
against their declared expected outputs, including checks that 
intentionally reproduce undesirable behavior. These counts 
measure agreement with function -level expectations. They do 
not measure t he fraction of the application that is correct. 
Database constraints, middleware execution, browser behavior, 
live model output, and concurrent requests are outside this test 
boundary.

Fig. 4 uses the constructed sequences [60, 70, 80], [80, 70, 
60], and [70, 70, 70]. The helper returns slopes of 10, −10, and

confirm the prediction clamps at both bounds and rejection of 
empty or singular inputs.

diamonds are next -index extrapolations. These values are not observations

Perfect fits in these examples follow from deliberately 
simple inputs. They provide no estimate of forecasting error. 
Assessing predictive use would require time -ordered held-out 
records, a stated target, and comparison with a simple baseline 
such as the m ost recent score. A valid study would separate 
students and assessment periods to avoid information leakage, 
which can inflate reported predictive performance [13]. 


C. Missing-mark sensitivity 
For Fig. 5, one constructed student has a recorded score of 
80/100. Between zero and four pending assessments, each with 
maximum 100 and a null obtained mark, are added. The class -
subject service excludes their maxima and continues to report 
80%. The acade mic-history service includes those maxima, 
producing 80, 40, 26.67, 20, and 16%. 
 
Fig. 5. Executed missing-mark fixture. One recorded 80/100 result is held fixed 
while pending rows are added. The divergence comes from denominator 
selection. 
The difference can alter a student’s apparent passing status. 
It arises before any language -model interpretation. A separate 
zero-mark check confirms that the class-subject service counts 
a recorded zero as an assessed result. Table I compares the 
inspected paths and identifies why a common chart label cannot 
guarantee a common statistic. 
D. Rubric and grading boundaries 
Fig. 6 evaluates a single criterion worth ten marks with three 
qualifying keywords. Constructed responses match zero, one, 
two, or all three keywords. The suggestions are 5, 5, 6.7, and 
10 marks; an empty response receives zero. Thus unrelated 
nonempty tex t can receive the same score as a response 
containing one relevant keyword. Teacher review is necessary 
for any use of these suggestions. 
 
Fig. 6. Executed deterministic rubric fallback with a three -keyword criterion. 
The half-mark floor applies to nonempty responses, including zero keyword 
matches. 
Another supplementary check evaluates the default grade 
lookup at 89.99, 89.995, and 90%. The function returns A+, P, 
and O, respectively. The middle input lies between the explicit 
upper bound of one interval and the lower bound of the next, so 
the fallback grade applies. This behavior concerns the default 
rules and unrounded input; database -configured rules can 
differ. Grading should use continuous intervals or a specified 
rounding policy before lookup. 
VII. DISCUSSION AND DEPLOYMENT REQUIREMENTS 
A. What the implementation establishes 
The source connects profiles, academic records, and 
candidate evidence through explicit services and role -specific 
interfaces. Its deterministic methods are simple enough to 
reproduce. Official-value flags and evidence metadata can help 
users inspect the b asis of a result. The executed examples 
establish how selected functions behave for controlled inputs, 
including boundary cases that ordinary dashboard screenshots 
would conceal. 
B. Consistency and authorization priorities 
The highest priority for academic interpretation is a common 
policy for pending work, absence, zero marks, and excused 
assessments. Each service should state its population, 
denominator, and weighting rule. A shared, tested aggregation 
policy would prevent  a student’s percentage from changing 
merely because a different screen calls a different service. The 
default grade-boundary gap requires a separate correction. 
Authorization requires route-by-route testing with unrelated 
users and assessment identifiers. Teacher -role membership 
alone cannot establish ownership of a requested rubric or 
submission. The analysis does not establish tenant isolation, 
document confiden tiality, or secure handling of all memory 
records. Those properties require executable integration and 
security tests against a configured deployment. 
C. Limits of model assistance and external evidence 
The student -generation path checks response length and a 
short list of suspicious strings before accepting model text. 
These checks cannot prove factual grounding or resistance to 
prompt injection. A response field named 
isGroundingVerified is an application assertion, not an 
independent validation result. Numeric claims should be 
checked against retrieved records, and narrative quality should 
be assessed with a labeled task set. Educational language -
model research emphasizes fact checking and human oversight 
[10]; fluency alone does not establish factual faithfulness [11]. 
Rubric fallback scores need particular caution because the 
keyword floor can reward unrelated content. A practical policy 
would mark unavailable qualitative evaluation for teacher 
completion instead of interpreting the heuristic as a validated 
grade. Candi date alignment has similar limits: repository 
metadata and self-reported projects require review, and missing 
academic evidence needs an explicit eligibility status. 
VIII. CONCLUSION 
The examined platform combines academic ledgers, student 
profiles, teacher workflows, and placement evidence in a role -


oriented web application. Source tracing identifies the active 
service boundaries and distinguishes them from older 
implementations in the repository. Two existing unit tests and 
16 supplementary checks reproduce the selected numerical 
behaviors. The results show both predictable trend calculations 
and consequential inconsistencies: pending marks change 
denominators across services, default grading has an interval 
gap, and the rubric fallback assigns a half -mark floor to 
nonempty answers. A common academic policy, complete 
record-level authorization, and independent evaluation of 
generated text are required before the implementation can 
support reliable institutional decisions.
